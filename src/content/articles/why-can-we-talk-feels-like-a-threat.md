Your phone lights up.

> Can we talk?

There’s no subject and no suggested time. Before you’ve written back, you’re replaying your last conversation. Maybe that joke landed badly. Maybe a decision has already been made and you’re about to hear it.

Sometimes the conversation is difficult. Sometimes it’s about Saturday’s plans. Right now, all you know is that someone wants to talk.

## Waiting for a conversation you know little about

Psychologists Dan Grupe and Jack Nitschke reviewed how people anticipate possible threats. Preparing for a difficult conversation can be useful. It’s harder to know what to prepare for when you have little information about what might happen, when it might happen or how serious it might be. [Grupe & Nitschke, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23783199/)

A message like “Can we talk?” can leave all of that open. The research helps explain why the wait can feel uncomfortable. The cited studies don’t establish that these particular words are a unique trigger. Feeling alarmed tells you how the message landed with you. You’ll need more information to know what the sender meant.

## What you bring to the message

The same words can land differently depending on who sent them and what’s happened between you. If your manager has used that phrase before a disciplinary meeting, that history gives you a reason to take it seriously.

Psychologists Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman studied **rejection sensitivity**, a tendency to anxiously expect rejection, readily perceive it and react strongly to it. In one experiment, people who expected rejection more anxiously were more likely to read ambiguous behaviour as intentional rejection. Their other studies also linked these expectations with how partners interpreted insensitive behaviour and with dissatisfaction in relationships. [Downey & Feldman, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8667172/)

In two later studies, Kathy Berenson and colleagues found that cues linked to rejection could disrupt attention on a task among people higher in rejection sensitivity. That finding comes from specific study tasks. It can help explain why a worrying cue might pull your attention back to it, although the researchers didn’t test what happens when this particular message arrives. [Berenson et al., 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2771869/)

Junwen Chen and colleagues found an association between social anxiety and a tendency to give ambiguous social events a negative meaning or expect especially bad consequences from mildly negative events. The association leaves causality open. One worried response to a message tells you very little about whether someone has a lasting pattern or a disorder. [Chen, Short & Kemps, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32777650/)

## If you’re the one sending it

Give the person a subject if you can. If you need time before you can explain, tell them when you’ll be able to talk. For a conversation about weekend plans, that could sound like this.

> Can we talk tonight about Saturday’s plans? Nothing urgent. I need about ten minutes.

Use “nothing urgent” when it’s true. If the subject is difficult, a plain description gives the other person a better idea of what they’re agreeing to.

## When asking for context feels unsafe

If someone has used silence or threatened conversations to control you, take that history seriously. You can ask for support, set a boundary or decline to meet alone. Choose what’s safe in that relationship.

A single worried reaction can happen to anyone. If fear of rejection repeatedly leads to hours of rumination, avoidance or strain in your relationships, a qualified professional can help you assess the pattern and consider what support fits.

## The truth to live by

The conversation may be difficult. If you have a good reason to expect that, use what you know to prepare. When the text itself is all you have, give yourself room to wait for the missing detail.

## Try writing three short lines

- **What I know.** Write down the exact words, who sent them and any timing they gave. Include relevant history you can point to.  
- **What I’m predicting.** Write down the explanation you’ve started rehearsing. “I’m predicting that they’re upset about yesterday” leaves room for an answer.  
- **What I can do.** Pick one step. You might ask what it’s about, check whether it’s urgent or say you need more context before you agree to meet.

This is a communication exercise for a moment of uncertainty. It hasn’t been validated as a treatment. If it’s safe to ask, start with one question you’d like answered.

> I can talk at six. What’s it about?
