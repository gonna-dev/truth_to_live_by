“We should do this more often.”

It is one of the warmest sentences in adult friendship and one of the least reliable plans. Both people may mean it. Neither person is being evasive. Then the evening ends, the calendar fills, and affection is asked to compete with everything that already has a date.

Earlier in life, friendship often borrows its structure from somewhere else. You share a classroom, a neighbourhood, a team, a workplace or a house. Seeing one another does not require a new decision every time. When that structure disappears, the friendship may lose contact without losing importance.

This is one reason adult friendships can fade so quietly. There may be no argument to repair and no moment when either person decides to leave. Good intentions simply fail to create the next occasion.

A ritual is one way to give closeness somewhere to happen.

The word can sound solemn. Here it means something ordinary and recognisable: the Sunday call, the first walk of the month, the voice note sent on the train home, the annual meal, the message after every match. A ritual reduces the number of times a friendship must be rescued by spontaneous organisation.

It does not prove that the relationship is deep. It makes depth easier to practise.

## Friendship has fewer automatic protections

Many adult roles arrive with expectations and reminders. Work produces meetings. Children have schedules. Households generate tasks. Romantic and family relationships are often acknowledged publicly and organised institutionally, even when the relationships themselves are complicated.

Friendship is usually more voluntary. That freedom is part of its value. You are not together because a contract, surname or rota requires it. But voluntary relationships can also be easier to postpone. There may be no recognised emergency when months of “soon” become years.

Research on friendship maintenance has identified recurring behaviours such as interaction, supportiveness, openness and positivity. These categories came from studies asking people how friendships are maintained; they are descriptions, not a recipe that guarantees closeness. They nevertheless point towards an important fact: friendship is not only a feeling two people possess. It is also something they do.

In a small 18-month study, Sam Roberts and Robin Dunbar followed 25 students moving from school to university. The emotional intensity of friendships was particularly sensitive to changes in contact frequency and in the number of activities friends did together. The sample is too small and specific to tell us how every friendship changes. Its result fits a familiar experience: relationships can remain loved in memory while becoming thinner in practice.

## Time matters, but it is not a stopwatch

Jeffrey Hall studied how new acquaintances became friends among recently relocated adults and first-year students. More time together was associated with greater closeness, and leisure time was especially relevant. The study has sometimes been reduced to a set of hour thresholds for making a friend.

That is more precision than the evidence can sensibly provide for an individual relationship. People differ. Activities differ. An hour of easy conversation is not interchangeable with an hour of required proximity. The more useful finding is simpler: closeness generally needs opportunities in which something more than coordination can occur.

A ritual protects some of those opportunities.

It might be frequent and small:

- a ten-minute call during a regular commute;
- sending one photograph from the week each Friday;
- walking together on the same morning each month.

It might be occasional and substantial:

- a yearly weekend in a place both people can reach;
- a meal around the date you first met;
- a seasonal day devoted to a shared interest.

The best form is not the one that looks most devoted. It is the one both people can reasonably return to.

## Repetition creates a place for more than updates

When contact is rare, a conversation can become an exchange of headlines: work, family, health, plans. There is so much to report that little time remains to inhabit an ordinary moment together.

Regularity lowers the cost of beginning. Not every meeting needs to justify the delay since the last one. Friends can discuss something trivial, change their minds, sit quietly or continue a conversation that never needed a formal opening.

This may be one reason routine maintenance matters. In four studies involving 1,325 participants, Melikşah Demir and colleagues found that friendship-maintenance behaviours helped explain the association between feeling that a friend supported one’s autonomy and reported happiness. The studies do not show that scheduling a call causes happiness. They suggest that the quality of a friendship and the behaviours that sustain it are connected.

A ritual is therefore not merely a calendar technique. At its best, it communicates: this relationship is allowed to occupy ordinary time.

## Do not turn care into attendance

Any practice that supports a relationship can also become a test the relationship repeatedly fails.

One friend misses two calls. The other begins counting. A monthly dinner becomes evidence in a private trial: if I mattered, they would have protected this. The ritual that was meant to reduce uncertainty starts producing it.

Reliability matters. Persistent one-sidedness can reveal a real difference in capacity, priority or desire. But a ritual should include a humane way to miss it. Illness, care, work, money, disability and simple exhaustion do not affect every person equally. A friendship can be important without both lives being equally flexible.

Before creating a ritual, agree on what it means when the plan fails. Can either person move it without apology? Does a missed month disappear, or must it be rescheduled? Is the form still useful in a difficult season?

The goal is continuity, not perfect attendance.

## Reciprocity is not identical contribution

Research on friendship maintenance also finds that perceptions of fairness and mutual investment matter. That does not mean each message must receive one message or every visit must be returned at the same distance.

Friends contribute differently. One remembers dates; another makes space when something goes wrong. One initiates more often; another is unusually dependable once a plan exists. Fairness in a friendship is better understood over time and in context than through an exact exchange.

Still, resentment deserves attention. If one person continually creates every occasion, adjusts every plan and carries every difficult conversation, the answer is not to call the arrangement a ritual and try harder. It may be time to say what maintaining the friendship currently requires and ask what each person can genuinely offer.

Rituals work best when they remove avoidable coordination, not when they conceal avoidable honesty.

## Give one friendship an address

Choose one relationship you would like to protect. Not the person you think you ought to choose—the person with whom continued closeness still feels alive and welcome.

Then make four decisions together:

1. **Choose a natural interval.** Weekly may be effortless for one friendship and impossible for another. Begin with reality.
2. **Choose a low-friction form.** Use a time, place or activity that already fits at least part of both lives.
3. **Choose a reason.** “Because we should” is weaker than “because I miss knowing the ordinary parts of your life.”
4. **Choose a return rule.** Decide how the ritual resumes after somebody misses it.

You might discover that the relationship does not need a recurring appointment. Some friendships survive long intervals and return with remarkable ease. Some are sustained through practical help, shared communities or bursts of contact during particular seasons. The aim is not to force every friendship into the same pattern.

It is to notice when a valued relationship is being left to an arrangement that no longer exists.

Adult friendship does not always need more intensity. Often it needs a next time. A ritual places that next time somewhere both people can find it.
