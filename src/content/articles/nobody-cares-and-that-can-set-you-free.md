You get someone’s name wrong in a meeting. A few seconds later, everyone’s talking about next week’s deadline.

Other people continue talking. You begin conducting an inquiry.

Did everyone notice? Should you apologise? Will it sound worse if you mention it now?

The moment took a few seconds. You can spend the rest of the meeting replaying it. How much of the meeting did everyone else spend thinking about it?

## The spotlight effect

Psychologists Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Husted Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky used the term **spotlight effect** for our tendency to overestimate how much other people notice our appearance and behaviour.

In one experiment, the researchers asked students to wear a T-shirt with a picture they expected the students to find embarrassing, then briefly enter a room of other students. The students wearing the shirt guessed how many people could identify the person pictured. Fewer observers could identify the face than the wearers expected.

Something similar happened when students chose shirts they felt good about wearing. In a group discussion, participants also overestimated how much their good and bad contributions stood out. They still had some sense of who had been more noticeable. A person who thought they stood out could be right about that and still overestimate by how much.

Several of the experiments had small groups of people whose appearance or behaviour was being judged. Most participants were North American students. The studies show that the effect can happen. They leave open how often it happens in daily life and how much it varies across cultures and settings. [Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211)

## What did they make of it?

You can be right that someone noticed you stumble over an answer and still be wrong about what they made of it.

Across four studies involving imagined blunders, public failure on an intellectual task and embarrassing descriptions, Kenneth Savitsky, Nicholas Epley and Thomas Gilovich found that people expected harsher judgments than observers reported. One explanation is that the person who stumbled kept thinking about the stumble. Observers had the rest of the performance to go on, along with whatever else they knew about the person. [Savitsky, Epley & Gilovich, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.1.44)

Later experiments found a similar pattern with changes in appearance, athletic performance and a video game. Participants expected their ups and downs to be more visible than they were. In one study, observers appeared to be partly occupied with what they were doing themselves. [Gilovich, Kruger & Medvec, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1006/jesp.2001.1490)

## When someone’s opinion matters

A job interviewer is there to assess you. A friend may remember a remark because it touched on something you’d talked about before. Prejudice, stigma and unequal power can give other people’s judgments real consequences.

Mark Leary and Robin Kowalski’s review distinguishes between what motivates us to make an impression and how we decide what impression to make. Both depend on who’s there, what’s at stake and what’s expected of us. Wanting a friend to hear the sincerity in an apology makes sense. Trying to work out what everyone thought of a passing remark can take up the rest of your afternoon. [Leary & Kowalski, 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.107.1.34)

David Kenny and Bella DePaulo’s review found that how we see ourselves strongly shapes how we think others see us. Their responses can give us more to go on. If you think you’ve upset a friend, what they say when you ask is more useful than another hour spent replaying the conversation. [Kenny & DePaulo, 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346325/)

## When fear of scrutiny keeps coming back

A 2026 review of 143 studies involving more than 80,000 people found a moderate link between fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety. The strength of the link varied considerably across studies. Most measured fear and anxiety at the same time, which leaves open how each influences the other. [Peng et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42850663/)

When you’re listening for a shake in your own voice, it can be harder to hear how the other person is responding. Researchers have proposed that this kind of inward attention may help sustain social anxiety. In one small speech experiment involving people diagnosed with generalised social phobia, increasing self-focus raised some anxiety measures. Effects on performance varied by measure. [Spurr & Stopa, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12238248/) · [Woody, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8666712/)

The spotlight effect describes one way people can misjudge how much others notice them. Recognising it does not diagnose or treat social anxiety. If fear of scrutiny repeatedly keeps you from taking part or interferes with daily life, a qualified professional can help you understand what’s happening and what support fits.

## The truth to live by

If you stumble over a sentence, you can carry on speaking. Let the conversation acquire another sentence.

If you cut someone off or missed a commitment, there’s something you can do. Give them room to finish. Acknowledge the missed promise and say what you’ll do about it. You can respond to what happened without settling what everyone in the room thinks of you.

## Try one small thing

Pick something harmless you’ve been putting off because you expect to feel watched. Ask a question in a meeting, wear something you like or go to an event on your own.

Beforehand, write down what you expect people to notice and how you expect them to respond. Be specific about what you could see or hear. “Someone will ask why I came alone” gives you something you can check. “Everyone will think I’m awkward” asks you to guess what’s going on in their heads.

Use this as a small reflection exercise. It has not been validated as a treatment and is not exposure therapy.

Afterwards, write down what you observed. If someone asked about your plans for the weekend, write that down. If you saw a look you couldn’t read, leave its meaning open. If your action affected someone, ask about it or make it right. Compare what you predicted with what you could actually see or hear.
